Algorithmic Extremism: Leaderless Radicalization and the New Landscape of Domestic Terrorism

by Jamie Sessions • University of Idaho

When discussing domestic threats to security, the landscape of modern terrorism looks drastically different than that of the previous century. Traditional acts of domestic terrorism in America once mirrored the hierarchical structures described by Bruce Hoffman, entailing established groups with central leadership and set orders to follow. A key feature of late 20th-century extremist groups was their reliance on tangible resources and discreet person-to-person contact, which made them significantly easier for federal authorities to monitor and infiltrate. However, the rise of the digital age has precipitated the emergence of diffuse, online-centric networks, effectively flattening the architecture of radicalization. Particularly disturbing is the rise of the "Manosphere." Using the framework proposed by J.M. Berger, these amorphous ideological movements can be defined as extremist because they view the success of their in-group (men) as being inseparable from hostile action against an out-group (women and feminists). This digital environment creates what researchers call radical filter bubbles, where algorithms start to curate an individualized extremist experience.

From its origins as an online support group, the Incel subculture has evolved into a significant instigator of domestic political violence. The community's radicalization is driven by what Ted Gurr identifies as relative deprivation, the psychological tension created by a perceived discrepancy between value expectations and value capabilities. In the Incel context, this manifests as a sense of aggrieved entitlement regarding social and sexual status, leading to a Blackpill nihilism where violence is presented as a justifiable reaction to social struggle. The Incel movement lacks a physical presence or official doctrine and instead, it relies on peer-to-peer radicalization on modern digital platforms. By decentralizing the pipeline, the movement has adapted the strategy of leaderless resistance, a concept historically associated with figures like Louis Beam but now perfected by anonymous digital actors. This creates a stochastic environment where individuals are independently inspired to act, challenging the conventional frameworks through which law enforcement agencies understand and disrupt mass violence.

The ideological devolution from formally organized groups to platform-guided, self-radicalizing networks threatens to outpace national security infrastructure. As radicalization becomes a private experience, the capacity for federal agencies to intervene is severely limited. Specifically, the central inquiry of this research is: How has the transition from hierarchical terrorist organizations to decentralized, 'leaderless' radicalization on digital platforms altered the lethality of domestic political violence in the U.S., and to what extent does this evolution signify a failure of traditional federal threat-management models to adapt to non-linear radicalization pathways?

Answering this question requires a multidisciplinary approach, combining sociological perspectives with tactical policy assessments. Understanding the role of modern online networks necessitates an analysis of radicalization through the "leaderless" framework, drawing on the Sageman-Hoffman debate regarding the transition from centralized command to ad-hoc networks. Furthermore, by analyzing specific Incel-related attacks, we can apply Andrew Kydd and Barbara Walter's strategies of terrorism to determine if a decentralized approach results in a higher frequency of scattered attacks or a shift toward high-casualty, single-attacker shootings. By examining shifts in Department of Justice and FBI doctrine, we see a clear mismatch between conventional crime-fighting methods, which prioritize structural change, and the realities of modern, non-linear digital extremism.

The central argument is that the move to decentralized online radicalization is not simply a byproduct of technology, but an organic strategic adaptation that circumvents federal control. While lone-wolf tactics may lack the coordinated destruction of events like the Oklahoma City bombing, they lead to more frequent and resilient instances of ideological violence. Because the Incel radicalization process thrives on the interplay between public and private digital spaces, federal counterterrorism methods, which heavily depend on disrupting organized groups and targeting leaders, are increasingly less useful in combating this new form of extremism.

Theoretical Framework: Aggrieved Entitlement & Extremism

To understand the modern Incel movement, one must first establish the psychological incentive for civil violence. Scholars have long argued that radicalization is not a random occurrence but a response to perceived systemic failure. Ted Gurr's foundational theory of "relative deprivation" (RD) provides the primary lens for this analysis. Gurr argues that violence is a function of the tension between an individual's value expectations—which are the goods and conditions of life to which they believe they are justifiably entitled—and their value capabilities, which are the goods and conditions they think they are capable of attaining or maintaining. When this gap widens, the resulting frustration often translates into political or social aggression.

In the context of the Incel (involuntary celibate) subculture, this relative deprivation is filtered through what modern researchers call aggrieved entitlement. While Gurr's original work often focused on economic or political status, more recent scholarship extends this to the "Manosphere," arguing that Incel radicalization is rooted in a perceived loss of social and sexual hierarchy. R. Guy suggests that this is not merely a personal issue but a collective "diagnosing" of a world where feminism has supposedly stripped men of their natural rights. The Blackpill ideology serves as the extreme conclusion of this deprivation. It is a nihilistic worldview that states society is fundamentally rigged against a specific class of men based on immutable physical characteristics.

"Extremism is defined as the belief that an in-group's success or survival is inseparable from hostile action against an out-group."
— J.M. Berger

J.M. Berger reframes this as not merely a subcultural issue or grievance but a formal extremist movement. Berger defines extremism as the belief that an "in-group's" success or survival is inseparable from hostile action against an "out-group." For Incels, the in-group identity is created through shared victimhood and physical "inferiority," while the out-group includes women, feminists, and "normative" society. By defining the movement through Berger's framework, it becomes clear that Incel-related violence is not the result of isolated mental health crises but a predictable outcome of an extremist identity built on Gurr's model of deprivation.

The organizational structure of domestic terrorism has shifted from the top-down models of the 20th century to a bottom-up digital insurgency. Bruce Hoffman outlines the traditional model of terrorism as a centralized, bureaucratic, and often state-like business. In Hoffman's view, traditional groups required a chain of command, a training infrastructure, and a clear set of strategic objectives. This hierarchical nature made them susceptible to structural change policies, where federal agencies could disrupt the movement by targeting its leadership or cutting off its funding.

However, the Incel movement has bypassed this structure entirely by adopting the strategy of "leaderless resistance." As J. Doroszczyk and the team of Lewis and Ingram observe, this strategy, originally popularized by figures like Louis Beam in the 1980s, designed a movement where individuals could act independently toward a shared goal without direct orders or centralized logistics. The modern digital age has almost perfected this concept by allowing ideological synchronization without physical proximity. This shift is central to the ongoing Sageman-Hoffman debate. While Hoffman argues that leadership and an organizational core still matter for large-scale lethality and strategic continuity, Marc Sageman contends that the leaderless jihad or "bunch of guys" model—small, autonomous cells or individuals—has become the primary cause of modern radicalization. The Incel movement serves as a modern case study in Sageman's favor, demonstrating that a potent ideology can mobilize violence without a central headquarters, a designated command, or formal doctrine.

The Digital Pipeline: Algorithmic Curation and grey-Zone Ecosystems

The pipeline through which an individual moves from relative deprivation to violent action has been fundamentally altered by the digital architecture of the 21st century. Unlike traditional recruitment, which required discreet, person-to-person contact, modern radicalization is often an individualized and private experience. A. Reed and colleagues describe this process through the lens of "radical filter bubbles." On platforms like Reddit and Bluesky, personalization algorithms prioritize engagement, often pushing users toward increasingly extreme content to keep them active on the site.

This creates a self-reinforcing loop where a user's grievances are validated by a large group of anonymous peers and amplified by the platform's own algorithm. A. M. Gallo points out that this 'digital echo chamber' is actually a blind spot for traditional intelligence. While federal agencies were built to infiltrate physical meetings or monitor financial transactions, they are less equipped to intervene in algorithmic radicalization that takes place in grey-zone platforms or encrypted forums. In this environment, the "recruiter" is no longer a human figurehead but a mathematical formula designed to maximize "out-group" hostility to ensure platform retention.

The foundational premise of this study is that modern decentralized radicalization should be understood not as truly leaderless, but rather as "leader-automated." While traditional terrorist organizations often require a charismatic human figurehead to issue directives and select targets, the digital world of the Manosphere has replaced the human recruiter with the automated personalization algorithm. In this model, the algorithm functions as a "virtual commander." As Berger states, extremism is defined by the belief that the in-group's survival necessitates hostile action against an out-group. The personalization algorithms described by Reed et al. automate this process by continuously refining the boundaries of the "in-group" and "out-group" through "radical filter bubbles."

This virtual command performs two critical functions that mimic traditional leadership: target selection and escalation of commitment. By amplifying content that generates significant engagement and controversy, the algorithm effectively singles out women and feminists for hostile criticism, portraying them as the sole antagonists responsible for the decline of the male group. It also directs their growing investment towards the Blackpill, which is a nihilistic ideological destination. Unlike the often political goals of 20th-century insurgencies, which Weinstein argues often sought material resources or governance, the Blackpill makes traditional negotiation impossible. It establishes a strategic path where the only logical conclusion to a "rigged" social reality is a violent exit or a "beta uprising," effectively synchronizing individual actions without the need for a centralized headquarters.

The algorithm provides the structural pathway for extremism, while the psychological drive comes from a modernized version of Gurr's theory of relative deprivation. In the modern Incel context, this appears as "aggrieved entitlement." Gurr originally argued that civil violence is a function of the gap between value expectations and value capabilities. However, in the digital age, this deprivation is no longer framed as just a lack of resources, but as a theft of status within a perceived social system or hierarchy. As Rachel Guy observes, Incel ideology "diagnoses" the world as a gynocentric dictatorship that has stripped men of their natural social and sexual hierarchy.

This sense of loss serves as the kinetic trigger for violence. The radicalization pipeline is further accelerated by what can be termed the "Validation Loop." Within these forums, the use of "E-bile"—which is hyper-misogynistic rhetoric—and "shitposting"—the act of posting absurd, off-topic, or intentionally provocative messages, images, or comments on social media platforms, usually to gain attention—acts as a social credit system. This environment gamifies the path to violence where users are rewarded with digital status, like Reddit's 'upvotes', for expressing increasingly extreme views. This loop transforms the psychological frustration of relative deprivation into a collective extremist identity where violent rhetoric is the primary currency of belonging. Consequently, an individual's transition from a "lurker" to a "perpetrator" is not triggered by a human order, but by the internalized pressure to validate their status within the digital echo chamber.

Tactical Lethality: Scattered Attacks and the sainthood Cycle

A critical question in this evolution is whether decentralization has increased or decreased the lethality of domestic violence. Applying the "Strategies of Terrorism" by Andrew Kydd and Barbara Walter, one can see that Incel-related violence often utilizes "intimidation" and "provocation." Because Incels are not trying to hold territory or change a specific law, unlike the insurgents described by Jeremy Weinstein, their violence is often meant to signal the "cost" of the social order they despise.

Malthaner, O'Connor, and Lindekilde introduce the concept of "scattered attacks," arguing that the collective dynamics of lone-actor terrorism create a different kind of lethality. While a decentralized movement may struggle to pull off a coordinated, high-logistics event like the 9/11 attacks, they produce a higher frequency of "low-tech, high-impact" events. Hamm and Spaaij support this, stating that the radicalization pathway for lone-wolf actors is often shorter, more volatile, and harder for authorities to intercept. For Incels, this typically translates into single-attacker mass shootings targeting "soft" targets. These attacks are "random" in nature, impossible to predict individually, yet statistically certain to occur given a large enough radicalized online population.

By comparing the 1995 Oklahoma City Bombing, the 2014 Isla Vista killings by Elliot Rodger, and the 2018 Toronto van attack by Alek Minassian, the research demonstrates the chronological and structural evolution of domestic threat architectures. McVeigh operated within a discernible physical milieu, the militia movement, which mirrored the bureaucratic and linear structures described by Bruce Hoffman. In this traditional model, terrorism is a "top-down" enterprise requiring a chain of command, a training infrastructure, and a clear set of strategic objectives. McVeigh's preparation involved renting a Ryder truck under an alias and purchasing two tons of ammonium nitrate fertilizer, creating physical "signatures" that allowed the FBI to trace the footprints within 48 hours. In the modern landscape, however, the "logistically heavy" attacker has been replaced by the "logistically invisible" actor.

Elliot Rodger represents the digital pivot toward stochastic, leader-automated violence. His 137-page manifesto, My Twisted World, serves as the foundational text of the modern movement because it provides what Rachel Guy identifies as a "social diagnostic." Rodger did not merely vent personal frustration; he constructed an "intellectual" framework for hate by diagnosing women's nature as inherently "evil" and "dysgenic." By framing his mass murder as a "punishment" for a collective social crime, Rodger provided the "Incel Script" that subsequent actors would follow. This transition signifies the move from Hoffman's bureaucratic terrorism to Sageman's "leaderless" model, where the "sainthood cycle" replaces the formal promotion within a rank-and-file organization.

The unique resilience of this decentralized model is best demonstrated by the 2018 Toronto van attack. Alek Minassian had no direct contact with Rodger, yet his actions were directly "commanded" by Rodger's digital legacy. Moments before his attack, Minassian posted to Facebook: "The Incel Rebellion has already begun! All hail the Supreme Gentleman Elliot Rodger!" For Minassian, the attack was not about territory or policy change; it was a bid for "sainthood" within the digital collective. He was participating in a "Validation Loop" where "going ER" (committing a mass killing in Rodger's style) is rewarded with eternal digital fame. Minassian utilized a rented van—a "low-tech, high-impact" weapon—to kill 10 people. This mirrors the "scattered attacks" described by Malthaner et al., where the collective dynamics of a digital milieu produce autonomous episodes of violence that are connected only by shared ideology and digital reverence.

Institutional Mismatch: The Paradox of Resilience & detection Lag

Finally, the literature reveals a significant gap between the reality of modern extremism and the federal infrastructure designed to combat it. A. M. Gallo traces the evolution of DOJ and FBI doctrine, noting a slow transition from "structural change" policies (which targeted groups like the KKK or organized militias) to "threat-management" strategies (which target specific individuals before they can act). The problem, as Gallo and Berger argue, is that federal agencies are still looking for "organizations" in an era of "movements." Traditional threat management relies on identifying a "chain of custody" for an idea or an order. However, because Incel radicalization is non-linear and ad-hoc, there is no "leadership" to decapitate. This signifies a potential failure of traditional models to adapt. As radicalization moves into the algorithmic shadows, the national security infrastructure, which Theda Skocpol notes is often slow to reform during periods of rapid social or technological revolution, remains geared toward a threat landscape that no longer exists.

A. M. Gallo identifies the traditional DOJ and FBI approach as the "Prosecutor" model, which is designed to target established organizations through structural change, decapitating leadership and disrupting funding. This model is inherently ill-suited for "stochastic terrorism," where the "recruiter" is a mathematical formula and the "organization" is an amorphous network of anonymous actors. This decentralization creates a "Paradox of Resilience." Traditional groups are vulnerable to leadership removal, but the Incel movement is "hydra-headed." When federal authorities or platform moderators attempt to ban a specific community, such as the 2017 ban of the r/incels subreddit, it does not end the radicalization process. Instead, it triggers a "migration" to more extreme, less regulated "grey-zone" platforms like Incels.is or encrypted forums. This institutional pattern leaves the national security infrastructure chasing a nonexistent hierarchy while the actual radicalization occurs in the algorithm.

The most significant barrier to federal threat management is the Poe's Law problem: the difficulty in distinguishing between sincere extremist intent and "shitposting." The "Algorithmic Incel Radicalization Correlation" (AIRC) dataset reveals that this irony is a strategic defense mechanism. Threads on Incels.is often oscillate between extreme calls for violence and humorous self-deprecation. For example, a thread titled "Marxism-Rodgerism. Opinions?" outlines a revolutionary manifesto while utilizing layers of subcultural irony to mask the threat's sincerity. This structured ecosystem is frequently shielded from external scrutiny by this layer of subcultural camouflage. "Shitposting" acts as a form of "stochastic noise" that drowns out the signal of an actual perpetrator. For an FBI agent monitoring a forum, the distinction between a "lurker" engaging in ironic misogyny and a "perpetrator" planning an attack is invisible. This creates a Detection Lag where federal agencies, relying on the identification of a "credible threat" through intent and capability, are paralyzed. By the time an individual moves from "shitposting" to a Facebook post announcing the "Incel Rebellion," the detection lag has already collapsed into the attack itself.

Conclusions and Policy Recommendations

The evolution of domestic terrorism from the hierarchical systems of the 20th century to the algorithmic insurgencies of the 21st represents more than a shift in tactics; it is a fundamental transformation of how people are radicalized. The transition from the "top-down" model, which is exemplified by Timothy McVeigh, to the leader-automated model of the Incel movement has effectively disconnected extremist violence from the physical and logistical signs that federal authorities were designed to detect. By replacing the human recruiter with the personalization algorithm, the modern Manosphere has created a self-sustaining ecosystem where aggrieved entitlement is systematically converted into violent action through digitized validation loops and Blackpill nihilism.

Ultimately, the current disconnect between the Department of Justice's "Prosecutor" model and the reality of unpredictable terrorism signifies a structural failure to adapt to a non-linear world. As long as national security infrastructure remains focused towards monitoring physical militias and tangible supply chains, it will continue to suffer from a "Detection Lag" that collapses only at the moment of an attack. To secure the domestic landscape, federal doctrine must move beyond the pursuit of non-existent hierarchies and toward the monitoring of digital operational signatures and algorithmic accountability.

Policy Recommendations:

  • Judicial Recognition under 18 USC § 2331 Federal agencies must realize misogynistic violence qualifies by statute as domestic terrorism under 18 USC § 2331. Instituting a stand-alone federal charge of "Domestic Terrorism" would allow the FBI to track and allocate resources to monitor fluid movements instead of looking for non-existent centralized leaders.
  • Algorithmic Accountability and Section 230 Reform Digital governance should focus on algorithmic accountability rather than content censorship. Reforming Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act to distinguish between "protected human speech" and "unprotected algorithmic promotion" would incentivize platforms to break apart the validation loops that prioritize extremist rhetoric. Additionally, mandatory algorithmic transparency would allow researchers to identify the specific "entry points" where radicalization begins.
  • Adaptive Intelligence and Behavioral Threat Assessments Federal intelligence must transition into adaptive intelligence to detect operational signatures before the detection lag collapses. This involves a shift from monitoring physical tripwires to behavioral threat assessments that identify the signatures of radicalization pathways. Investigators must be trained to recognize the transition from subcultural "shitposting" to sincere operational language.
  • Disengagement and Sociocultural Interventions Countering violent extremism must include sociocultural efforts aimed at mitigating the psychological motivation of relative deprivation. Programs should focus on "disengagement" rather than "deradicalization." Creating alternative online spaces for disenfranchised men and publicly deconstructing the "social diagnostics" of Incel ideology can strip the movement of its legitimacy and defuse the radicalization pipeline.

Appendix E: Extremism Ranking Rubric for the AIRC Dataset

The rubric is designed to quantify ideological extremity, ranging from benign interactions to explicit operational directives or genocide advocacy.

Score Definition
0 The post is neutral, mundane, or unrelated to ideology, politics, or any group. No hate, no dehumanizing language, no threats, and no approval of violence.
1 Slightly edgy, hostile, or rude tone, but no clear targeting of protected groups or ideological enemies. No endorsement of harm or discrimination.
2 Contains stereotypes, mild insults, or dismissive language toward a group or ideology, but does not advocate harm, serious discrimination, or dehumanization.
3 Clearly hostile or resentful toward a group or ideology, may include stronger slurs or aggressive language, but still stops short of dehumanization or explicit calls for harm.
4 Openly hateful toward a group (e.g., women, minorities, political opponents), including degrading or dehumanizing language, but no explicit suggestion of violence or extermination.
5 Mix of strong hate and vague or indirect references to harm (e.g., "they deserve what happens to them," "someone should deal with them") without clear, direct incitement to violence.
6 Implies or fantasizes about violence or severe harm, endorses structural oppression or severe discrimination (e.g., segregation, removal of rights), but avoids explicit, direct commands to attack or kill.
7 Clearly supports or praises violence against a target group or individuals (e.g., saying attacks are good or justified) but may not directly call the reader to commit violence.
8 Directly encourages others to commit violence or serious harm, or explicitly supports organized extremist or terrorist groups, but without detailed operational plans.
9 Strong, explicit extremist ideology with clear calls for serious violence or ethnic/gender-based cleansing, possibly including suggestions of how or when, but not a detailed step-by-step plan.
10 Fully explicit extremist propaganda that strongly advocates large-scale violence, genocide, or terrorism, including clear targets and intent, and may include operational-style detail or praise of past attacks.
