  <section id="chap-intro" class="monograph-chapter active">
    <div class="chapter-title-header">
      <span class="chapter-number-tag">Section I</span>
      <h2>He Was on the Ballot</h2>
    </div>

    <div class="primer-card">
      <div class="primer-card-header">
        <span class="primer-card-badge">💡 The Big Picture</span>
        <span style="font-size: 0.8rem; color: var(--text-muted);">Reading Lens</span>
      </div>
      <div class="primer-card-title">What is a midterm, and why does it usually hurt the president?</div>
      <div class="primer-card-body">
        Every two years, Americans elect the whole House of Representatives and about a third of the Senate. When that happens halfway through a president's term, the president isn't on the ballot, but the election almost always turns into a verdict on them anyway. The president's party nearly always loses seats. In 2026 the question wasn't whether Republicans would lose, but how badly and why.
        <div class="primer-analogy">
          <strong>Key Concept:</strong> A <span class="term-chip" data-term="midterm_election">midterm election</span> is the country's built-in check on whoever holds the White House.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <p>
      Before the 2026 midterms, Donald Trump urged his supporters to "vote like I'm on the ballot." After Republicans lost both chambers of Congress, he complained that he had not been on it: "If I was, we would have won IN A LANDSLIDE!" (<a href="#ref-northeastern" class="citation-link">Northeastern Global News 2026</a>). Preliminary NBC exit polling points the other way: 59 percent of voters said Trump influenced their vote, with 43 percent voting to reject him and only 16 percent voting to support him (<a href="#ref-cibercuba" class="citation-link">CiberCuba 2026</a>). Trump was not formally on the ballot, but his leadership and agenda were part of the electoral choice, and for many voters they appear to have been a reason to vote against his party.
    </p>

    <div class="case-card-quote" style="margin: 1.75rem 0; border-left-color: var(--accent-color); font-size: 1.05rem;">
      <strong>59% of voters said Trump influenced their vote. 43% voted to reject him; 16% voted to support him.</strong>
    </div>

    <p>
      The scale of that loss is hard to overstate. At the time of writing, Democrats have won 237 House seats and lead in 24 more, while Republicans have won 147 and lead in 27. In the Senate, Democrats hold 54 seats and lead in 2 more, against 44 for Republicans (<a href="#ref-aljazeera-a" class="citation-link">Al Jazeera 2026a</a>). The party needed four Senate seats to win a majority and has taken seven.
    </p>

    <p>
      The 2026 midterms offer grounds for criticizing Trump's leadership, but not for reducing every Republican loss to a single cause. Preliminary results suggest that dissatisfaction with his administration contributed to Democratic gains, alongside turnout differences, candidate quality and local conditions. They also suggest that voter switching played a real part, which would make this closer to a <span class="term-chip" data-term="persuasion_election">persuasion election</span> than most of the last decade's contests. That would mean Republicans lost support rather than merely failed to mobilize it. The available evidence does not yet establish how much of the shift came from persuasion rather than differences in turnout. The central question is therefore not whether Trump alone caused the outcome, but how far his leadership and agenda weakened Republican candidates.
    </p>

    <p>
      The strongest alternative explanation deserves attention first. A president's party almost always loses seats in a midterm, and Republican losses alone do not establish that voters specifically rejected Trump. A stronger case requires evidence that dissatisfaction with his administration contributed to the result beyond the effects of turnout, candidate quality and local conditions. Three kinds of evidence point in that direction: swings in places Trump won comfortably in 2024 that are too large to be easily explained by turnout, shifts among groups he had recently gained, and exit-poll measures linking vote choice to his record on prices, tariffs and the war. Each is preliminary. The sections below separate what the evidence reports, what can reasonably be inferred from it, and where the argument rests on judgment.
    </p>

    <p>
      If that reading holds, the Democratic majority looks closer to a loan than a realignment. Voters appear to have handed Democrats power to check the administration while showing little affection for the party receiving it. What Democrats do with a loan they may not have fully earned will shape the road to 2028.
    </p>

    <div class="chapter-title-header">
      <span class="chapter-number-tag">Section II</span>
      <h2>The Size of the Wave</h2>
    </div>

    <div class="primer-card">
      <div class="primer-card-header">
        <span class="primer-card-badge">💡 The Big Picture</span>
      </div>
      <div class="primer-card-title">How big was it, compared with other wave elections?</div>
      <div class="primer-card-body">
        Political analysts call an election a "wave" when one party gains a large number of seats all at once. Democrats' 2026 House gain is bigger than their waves in 2006 and 2018, though still smaller than the Republican wave of 2010. It also beat the polls: Democrats were expected to win, just not by this much, and they won governors' races in states like Texas and Ohio.
        <div class="primer-analogy">
          <strong>Key Concept:</strong> The <span class="term-chip" data-term="generic_ballot">generic ballot</span> asks voters which party they'd pick for Congress. Democrats led it by 8.9 points going in.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <p>
      The result needs some historical perspective before any explanation of it. Republicans entered the election holding the House 218 to 214 (<a href="#ref-northeastern" class="citation-link">Northeastern Global News 2026</a>). If every current leader holds on, Democrats will finish with 261 seats, a net gain of about 47, which sits within the 251 to 268 range estimated by CBS News (<a href="#ref-cbs-b" class="citation-link">CBS News 2026b</a>). That gain would exceed the 31 seats Democrats won in 2006 and the 40 they won in 2018, while falling short of the 63 Republicans won in 2010. A seven-seat Senate gain places 2026 in the company of 1986, 1994 and 2014, three of the largest midterm Senate swings of recent decades.
    </p>

    <p>
      The outcome also went beyond what most observers expected, even though a Democratic win was widely predicted. Just before the election, the RealClearPolitics <span class="term-chip" data-term="generic_ballot">generic ballot</span> average showed Democrats ahead by 8.9 points, and the FiftyPlusOne model gave them a 79 percent chance of winning both chambers. Nate Silver's Senate model gave Democrats an edge in Alaska, Iowa, Kansas, Maine, Michigan, North Carolina, Ohio and Texas (<a href="#ref-newsweek" class="citation-link">Newsweek 2026</a>). South Carolina was not on that list, and it flipped anyway. Steve Scalise conceded that the result "was far worse than any of us expected" (<a href="#ref-cbs-b" class="citation-link">CBS News 2026b</a>).
    </p>

    <p>
      The same current ran through the state level. Before the election there were 24 Democratic governors and 26 Republican governors. In 2027 there will be at least 29 Democrats and 20 Republicans, with Kansas still uncalled. Democrats flipped Alaska, Georgia, Iowa, Nevada, Ohio and Texas, where Gina Hinojosa defeated Greg Abbott, and Republicans flipped nothing (<a href="#ref-multistate" class="citation-link">MultiState 2026</a>). Governors' races usually give voters more room to split their tickets and judge candidates on local grounds, so these flips are significant, although each also turned on its own candidates and conditions. Taken together, they suggest that the anger was national enough to reach offices with no direct role in the war, the tariffs or immigration enforcement. They also hand Democrats a new group of executives from red and purple states just as the party begins looking toward 2028.
    </p>

  <section id="chap-moved" class="monograph-chapter">
    <div class="chapter-title-header">
      <span class="chapter-number-tag">Section III</span>
      <h2>The Voters Who Moved</h2>
    </div>

    <div class="primer-card">
      <div class="primer-card-header">
        <span class="primer-card-badge">💡 The Big Picture</span>
      </div>
      <div class="primer-card-title">Did Democrats win because their voters showed up, or because Trump's voters switched?</div>
      <div class="primer-card-body">
        There are two ways to win an election: get more of your own supporters to vote, or change people's minds. Political scientists have theories for both. In 2026, the swings in places Trump had won easily were too large to explain by turnout alone. Even Vice President Vance said many people who voted for Trump in 2024 voted for Democrats this time.
        <div class="primer-analogy">
          <strong>Key Concepts:</strong> <span class="term-chip" data-term="surge_decline">Surge and decline</span> says the president's casual voters stay home. <span class="term-chip" data-term="referendum_theory">Referendum theory</span> says voters actively punish the president.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <p>
      Some of the strongest preliminary evidence for persuasion comes from the places Republicans thought were safe. Democrats flipped Senate seats in Texas, North Carolina, South Carolina, Ohio, Kansas, Iowa and Maine, and they won House districts that Trump carried by as much as 18 points in 2024 (<a href="#ref-pbs" class="citation-link">PBS NewsHour 2026</a>). James Talarico became the first Democrat to win statewide in Texas in 32 years, and South Carolina counties that Trump won swung left by an average of about 21 points (<a href="#ref-northeastern" class="citation-link">Northeastern Global News 2026</a>). Republicans spent hundreds of millions of dollars defending states Trump had won by double digits only two years earlier (<a href="#ref-cbs-a" class="citation-link">CBS News 2026a</a>). Swings that large are difficult to explain through turnout alone. County and district results cannot by themselves separate voters who switched from voters who stayed home, however, so they support the persuasion argument without proving it.
    </p>

    <p>
      Political science offers two classic explanations for why a president's party loses seats in a midterm. Angus Campbell's (<a href="#ref-campbell" class="citation-link">1960</a>) <span class="term-chip" data-term="surge_decline">surge and decline</span> theory holds that the less engaged voters who turn out in presidential years simply stay home two years later. Edward Tufte (<a href="#ref-tufte" class="citation-link">1975</a>) instead treats the midterm as a <span class="term-chip" data-term="referendum_theory">referendum on the president</span> and the economy. On the evidence available so far, the 2026 results fit Tufte's account better, though not to the exclusion of Campbell's. Vice President JD Vance reached a similar conclusion in public, writing that "the main problem is not turnout" and that many people who voted for Trump and Vance in 2024 voted for Democrats this year (<a href="#ref-cbs-b" class="citation-link">CBS News 2026b</a>). His statement is an interpretation, not evidence, and it should be tested against the voter data rather than accepted on his authority.
    </p>

    <!-- Editorial Figure: I Voted stickers -->
    <figure class="monograph-figure">
      <img src="{{ '/assets/images/i-voted-stickers.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Sheets of red, white and blue 'I Voted' stickers" loading="lazy">
      <figcaption class="monograph-caption">
        <span><strong>Figure 3.1:</strong> Turnout rose slightly, but switching did most of the work.</span>
        <span>Photo: Joe Hall, <a href="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0/">CC BY 2.0</a>, via <a href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:I_Voted_stickers_sheet.jpg">Wikimedia Commons</a></span>
      </figcaption>
    </figure>

    <p>
      The preliminary exit data is consistent with that reading. Voters under 30 were roughly split between Trump and Kamala Harris in 2024, but about 7 in 10 backed Democrats for the House this year (<a href="#ref-ap" class="citation-link">AP Voter Poll 2026</a>). Men, whom Trump won by double digits, largely split their votes, and majorities of Latinos and young voters returned to Democrats (<a href="#ref-depinto" class="citation-link">De Pinto and Backus 2026</a>). Among Latino registered voters who supported Trump in 2024, his approval fell from 93 percent in February 2025 to 57 percent this fall (<a href="#ref-pew" class="citation-link">Pew Research Center 2026</a>). Turnout still played a role: the electorate was slightly more Democratic than in the last two elections, and early voting surged in several competitive states (<a href="#ref-depinto" class="citation-link">De Pinto and Backus 2026</a>; <a href="#ref-yahoo" class="citation-link">Yahoo News 2026</a>). Enthusiasm likely added to the margin. Exit polls suggest that switching did more than turnout to put Kansas and Texas in play, but they cannot settle the question; that will require voter files and panel data.
    </p>

    <div class="case-card" style="margin: 1.75rem 0; border-left: 4px solid var(--accent-navy);">
      <div class="case-card-header">
        <span class="case-card-title">Who moved: 2024 vs. 2026</span>
        <span class="case-card-tag">Exit Polls</span>
      </div>
      <ul style="margin: 0.5rem 0 0.25rem 1.25rem; font-size: 0.95rem; line-height: 1.7;">
        <li><strong>Voters under 30:</strong> roughly even in 2024 → about 7 in 10 Democratic for the House.</li>
        <li><strong>Men:</strong> Trump by double digits in 2024 → largely split.</li>
        <li><strong>Latino 2024 Trump voters:</strong> his approval fell from 93% (Feb. 2025) to 57% (fall 2026).</li>
        <li><strong>South Carolina Trump counties:</strong> swung left by about 21 points on average.</li>
      </ul>
    </div>

    <p>
      Redistricting offers a useful, if indirect, test. Republicans redrew maps in the middle of the decade hoping for a net gain of about 15 seats, and Newsweek found that they had lost all nine of the redrawn districts Cook rated as competitive or leaning Democratic (<a href="#ref-cameron" class="citation-link">Cameron 2026</a>). Florida is the sharpest example. Its new map targeted four Democratic seats, three of which Trump would have carried by double digits, yet Kathy Castor held her district after it swung nearly 16 points toward Democrats from its 2024 presidential result (<a href="#ref-aljazeera-b" class="citation-link">Al Jazeera 2026b</a>). A <span class="term-chip" data-term="gerrymander">gerrymander</span> is a bet that a Trump voter in 2024 is a Republican voter in 2026. Mapmakers used last election's numbers to spread their supporters across more districts, which left those districts exposed once the numbers moved. California's own redraw and the small shift in the median seat limited what the maps could have done anyway (<a href="#ref-cameron" class="citation-link">Cameron 2026</a>), but the results are consistent with a simple lesson: a map built on one election's numbers offers little protection if voters change their minds.
    </p>

  </section>

  <section id="chap-giant" class="monograph-chapter">
    <div class="chapter-title-header">
      <span class="chapter-number-tag">Section IV</span>
      <h2>The Swinging Giant</h2>
    </div>

    <div class="primer-card">
      <div class="primer-card-header">
        <span class="primer-card-badge">💡 The Big Picture</span>
      </div>
      <div class="primer-card-title">Why Latino voters swung so far, so fast</div>
      <div class="primer-card-body">
        In 2024, Latino voters moved sharply toward Trump, and many commentators wondered whether they were becoming Republicans for good. Two years later, many moved right back. Aggressive immigration enforcement in their own communities and rising prices both played a part. But "Latino voters" is not one group: Cuban Americans in Miami and Puerto Ricans in Orlando voted very differently.
        <div class="primer-analogy">
          <strong>Key Concept:</strong> The <span class="term-chip" data-term="swinging_giant">"swinging giant"</span> describes a large group of voters who move sharply between parties rather than staying loyal to one.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <p>
      No group shows the volatility of the current electorate better than Latino voters. Their movement toward Trump in 2024 was widely described as a possible realignment. He won all but two Texas border counties that year and flipped Cameron and Hidalgo for the first time in decades (<a href="#ref-birenbaum" class="citation-link">Birenbaum 2026</a>). Two years later, Hinojosa carried Hidalgo County 70.5 percent to 28.5 percent. In Miami-Dade County, Rep. Carlos Gimenez won by 7 points after winning by 29 in 2024 (<a href="#ref-paul" class="citation-link">Paúl and Morin 2026</a>). Stephanie Valencia offered the phrase that may outlast this election: "People called us the sleeping giant for so long, and now it's clear that we're the <span class="term-chip" data-term="swinging_giant">swinging giant</span>."
    </p>

    <p>
      Immigration enforcement appears to be the most visible reason for the shift. Early this year, federal immigration officers killed two U.S. citizens, Renee Good and Alex Pretti, during the crackdown in Minneapolis. AP reporting from Colorado's 8th District, where about 4 in 10 voters are Hispanic, found every Hispanic voter interviewed offended by the crackdown, nine months before the election (<a href="#ref-riccardi" class="citation-link">Riccardi 2026</a>). ICE made more than 63,000 arrests in Texas and about 29,000 in Florida between January and August, and an ICE officer shot and killed Lorenzo Salgado Araujo in Houston (<a href="#ref-paul" class="citation-link">Paúl and Morin 2026</a>). Many of these voters had supported stricter border policy in 2024. The reporting suggests that what many of them saw in 2026 was enforcement reaching their own neighborhoods, workplaces and relatives.
    </p>

    <p>
      Enforcement alone does not explain the swing, however, and treating Latino voters as a single immigration constituency would repeat an old mistake. Texas Latino voters were also reacting to the cost of living, particularly gas prices after the attack on Iran (<a href="#ref-birenbaum" class="citation-link">Birenbaum 2026</a>). Florida shows how uneven the movement was. VoteHub's precinct analysis found that Cuban-majority precincts still favored Republican Ashley Moody by 7.5 points, while Puerto Rican precincts favored Democrat Angie Nixon by 33 (<a href="#ref-paul" class="citation-link">Paúl and Morin 2026</a>). The category hides several different electorates with different histories.
    </p>

    <p>
      The swing should concern Democrats as much as it encourages them. Democratic strategist Chuck Rocha explained the return simply: "They're swinging back because Democrats are reminding them who broke it" (<a href="#ref-birenbaum" class="citation-link">Birenbaum 2026</a>). That is an explanation built on blame. It says little about loyalty, and the Pew finding that only 42 percent of Latino Democratic leaners feel the party cares about them points the same way. A group that moved this far toward Republicans in 2024 and this far away in 2026 has shown that it is willing to move again. Whichever party holds power when prices next rise should expect to meet the same voters from the other side.
    </p>

    </div>

    <div class="primer-card">
      <div class="primer-card-header">
        <span class="primer-card-badge">💡 The Big Picture</span>
      </div>
      <div class="primer-card-title">It was the prices, and the war showed up at the pump</div>
      <div class="primer-card-body">
        Trump won in 2024 by promising to bring prices down. By 2026, gas cost more than $4 a gallon, tariffs had raised the cost of goods, and health insurance premiums were set to jump. The war with Iran mattered mainly because it pushed up fuel prices. Voters also worried about corruption and immigration enforcement. But they weren't especially excited about Democrats either; they mostly wanted to send a message.
        <div class="primer-analogy">

    <p>
      The available evidence suggests that prices were the leading reason voters moved. In the NBC exit poll, 79 percent said inflation was causing hardship for their family, 62 percent held Trump responsible for high prices, and 65 percent said his tariffs harmed the country (<a href="#ref-cibercuba" class="citation-link">CiberCuba 2026</a>). His 38 percent approval among voters compares with 45 percent in the 2018 midterms, when his party lost the House. Trump campaigned in 2024 on fixing the cost of living, which gave voters a clear standard for judging him. By the exit polls' measure, most of them concluded he had not met it.
    </p>

    <p>
      The Iran war deserves a more careful reading than it has received. Vance declared that "the reason we lost is the war," and Trump agreed it was "largely true" because the war drove up gasoline prices (<a href="#ref-cbs-b" class="citation-link">CBS News 2026b</a>). Most voters did call the military action the wrong decision (<a href="#ref-depinto" class="citation-link">De Pinto and Backus 2026</a>). In the AP Voter Poll, however, few voters named foreign policy as their top issue, and the war ranked behind cost of living and corruption as a crucial factor (<a href="#ref-ap" class="citation-link">AP Voter Poll 2026</a>). The polling suggests the war mattered mostly as a price that people paid at the pump. It may also have broken an America First promise that many of Trump's newer voters took seriously. Vance's explanation emphasizes a single policy decision rather than broader dissatisfaction with the administration. That interpretation should be evaluated against the survey evidence on tariffs, immigration enforcement and corruption, rather than accepted on the basis of the election results alone. As a matter of interpretation, it could also help distance the administration's wider agenda from the result, although that political advantage does not establish whether the explanation is accurate.
    </p>

    <p>
      The path of gasoline prices shows how that happened. The national average stood at $2.98 per gallon on February 28, around the start of the war, and reached $4.56 in May. On Election Day it was $4.15, the first time the Election Day average had been above $4, compared with $3.80 in 2022 and $2.75 in 2018 (<a href="#ref-folk" class="citation-link">Folk 2026</a>). Iran blockaded the Strait of Hormuz, Trump's ceasefire collapsed over the summer, and the emergency stockpile fell to its lowest level in 44 years. Diesel topped $6 per gallon, all of this after Trump had pledged "gasoline below $2 a gallon" (<a href="#ref-philbrick" class="citation-link">Philbrick 2026</a>).
    </p>

      Gasoline has a political quality that other prices lack. A voter may not know the inflation rate, but the price of gas is posted in large numbers along every commute, and in 2026 it could be traced to a specific decision by a specific person. Diesel carries the cost further into the economy, since it moves crops, machinery and freight. That helps explain why the revolt reached farm states and rural counties that have little else in common with suburban Democrats. Philbrick reports New York Times and Siena polling in which voters in five red states disliked Trump's handling of gas prices more than any other issue. Aaron Lehman, head of the Iowa Farmers Union, said his members "voted like Trump was on the ballot" (<a href="#ref-cbs-b" class="citation-link">CBS News 2026b</a>). Rural voters had been among the most reliable parts of the Trump coalition, and tariffs and diesel reached them at the same time.
    </p>

    <p>
      The survey data suggests that dissatisfaction extended well beyond the war. Most voters said immigration efforts went too far (<a href="#ref-depinto" class="citation-link">De Pinto and Backus 2026</a>). About 3 in 10 named the state of democracy as their top issue, roughly half called government corruption crucial to their vote, and about 6 in 10 said Trump puts his own interests above the country's (<a href="#ref-ap" class="citation-link">AP Voter Poll 2026</a>). Candidates made the problem worse in some races. Ken Paxton brought corruption allegations and a 2023 impeachment by his own party into the Texas race, and political scientist Martha Johnson points to Republican candidate quality as a real factor (<a href="#ref-northeastern" class="citation-link">Northeastern Global News 2026</a>).
    </p>

    <p>
      Health care worked as a second cost-of-living issue. Only about 1 in 10 voters named it as their top concern, but it was among the widest Democratic advantages in the AP poll (<a href="#ref-ap" class="citation-link">AP Voter Poll 2026</a>). The 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act added new work requirements for many adults on Medicaid, enhanced Affordable Care Act subsidies expired, and benchmark premiums are set to rise by an average of 13 percent for 2027 (<a href="#ref-armour" class="citation-link">Armour and Seitz 2026</a>). This gave Democrats a concrete way to connect a Republican law to a monthly bill. Together with gas and tariffs, it allowed the party to run on one simple charge: that the president who promised to lower costs had raised them.
    </p>

    <p>
      What is missing from all of this is any great enthusiasm for Democrats. Just over half of voters viewed the party unfavorably in the CBS exit poll (<a href="#ref-depinto" class="citation-link">De Pinto and Backus 2026</a>), and just under half viewed it favorably in the AP poll (<a href="#ref-ap" class="citation-link">AP Voter Poll 2026</a>). Their edge on the economy was slight. Before the election, only 42 percent of Latino voters leaning Democratic said the party cares a great deal or quite a bit about people like them (<a href="#ref-pew" class="citation-link">Pew Research Center 2026</a>). Abramowitz and Webster (<a href="#ref-abramowitz" class="citation-link">2016</a>) describe <span class="term-chip" data-term="negative_partisanship">negative partisanship</span> as voters being driven by dislike of the other side. The twist in 2026 is that the dislike reached people who had recently been on Trump's side. Martha Johnson's warning is the right one: this cannot be read as "a clear victory for the Democratic Party's policy agenda" (<a href="#ref-northeastern" class="citation-link">Northeastern Global News 2026</a>).
    </p>

  <section id="chap-calcification" class="monograph-chapter">
    <div class="chapter-title-header">
      <span class="chapter-number-tag">Section VI</span>
      <h2>Why Calcification Did Not Hold</h2>
    </div>

    <div class="primer-card">
      <div class="primer-card-header">
        <span class="primer-card-badge">💡 The Big Picture</span>
      </div>
      <div class="primer-card-title">If Americans are so locked into their parties, how did this happen?</div>
      <div class="primer-card-body">
        A popular theory says American voters have become so loyal to their party that almost nothing changes their votes anymore. That's mostly true for die-hard partisans. But Trump's 2024 win depended on newer, less committed supporters who signed on for specific promises. When those promises weren't kept, they left. Money, maps and rallies couldn't bring them back.
        <div class="primer-analogy">
          <strong>Key Concepts:</strong> <span class="term-chip" data-term="calcification">Calcification</span> describes hardened party loyalty. <span class="term-chip" data-term="conditional_supporters">Conditional supporters</span> are voters whose loyalty depends on promises being kept.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <p>
      The size of these swings poses a problem for one of the most influential accounts of modern American politics. John Sides, Michael Tesler and Lynn Vavreck (<a href="#ref-sides" class="citation-link">2022</a>) describe a <span class="term-chip" data-term="calcification">calcified electorate</span>, in which partisans have become so fixed in their loyalties that even major events produce only small changes in vote choice. For much of the last decade that description fit well. Presidential elections were decided by narrow margins, scandals seemed to move few voters, and campaigns increasingly focused on turning out their own side. An election in which South Carolina counties move about 21 points and Hidalgo County reverses itself is difficult to reconcile with a fully calcified electorate.
    </p>

    <p>
      One plausible answer is that calcification was always uneven. It describes committed partisans better than it describes the voters who made Trump's 2024 win possible. Trump expanded his coalition among young voters, Latinos and men with a specific offer: lower prices, no new wars and an America First approach to the world. If this account is right, those were <span class="term-chip" data-term="conditional_supporters">conditional supporters</span> who had agreed to a deal, and a deal can be cancelled. Vance's own list of why 2024 Trump voters defected reads almost like a list of broken terms. He named ending the Iran war, the cost of living, America First and the relationship with Israel (<a href="#ref-cbs-b" class="citation-link">CBS News 2026b</a>). Trump's 38 percent approval shows the other half of the picture. A hard core of support remains, and it did not collapse the way it might in a less polarized era. What Republicans lost was the margin outside that core, and in a closely divided country the margin is the majority.
    </p>

    <p>
      This would also help explain why the usual Republican tools failed. Money did not stop it: the party spent hundreds of millions defending red states (<a href="#ref-cbs-a" class="citation-link">CBS News 2026a</a>). Maps did not stop it either, as the redistricting results showed. Nor did Trump's own campaigning. He had asked supporters to treat the election as a vote on him, and afterward he said his rallies had "such love" but "we weren't able to transfer the love to candidates" (<a href="#ref-cbs-b" class="citation-link">CBS News 2026b</a>). He meant it as a complaint about his candidates. Read alongside the exit polls, in which most voters said he influenced their vote and far more voted to oppose him than to support him, it points to a different problem. To the extent that Republican candidates tied their campaigns to Trump's leadership and agenda, their losses raise questions about the electoral strength of that strategy. Establishing that for particular races would require evidence of how closely each candidate aligned with him, through endorsements, messaging and policy positions, and the claim should not be stretched to cover every Republican loss. It does suggest that personal loyalty to Trump may be hard to pass on to other candidates, and possibly to a successor in 2028.
    </p>

    <div class="case-card-quote" style="margin: 1.75rem 0; border-left-color: var(--accent-color); font-size: 1.05rem;">
      <strong>"We weren't able to transfer the love to candidates."</strong> — Donald Trump, after the election
    </div>

    <p>
      The transfer problem has been visible for some time, since Trump-era Republicans have repeatedly performed worse without him at the top of the ticket. The 2026 result adds something new. In earlier midterms the explanation was that his voters stayed home. This time, by the vice president's own account, many of them came out and voted the other way. If the voter data confirms that account, Republicans face a harder problem. A party can try to fix a turnout problem with better organization and more rallies. Winning back people who have already crossed over would be a much harder job, because it requires changing what they think the party has done to them.
    </p>

    <h3 style="font-family: var(--font-serif); font-size: 1.35rem; margin-top: 2.25rem;">The Limits of the Persuasion Argument</h3>

    <p>
      The persuasion argument has real limits, and the evidence available this soon after an election cannot fully settle it. Exit polls and voter surveys are estimates, some of the figures cited here are preliminary, and many races are still being counted. The best evidence on switching will come from voter files and panel studies, which take months to appear. Those sources can separate two groups that look alike in an exit poll: people who voted for Trump in 2024 and for Democrats in 2026, and people whose turnout simply changed between the two elections. Campbell's <span class="term-chip" data-term="surge_decline">surge and decline</span> theory may still explain part of the result. Among early voters in 27 states reporting party registration, 43.1 percent were registered Democrats and 31.4 percent were Republicans (<a href="#ref-yahoo" class="citation-link">Yahoo News 2026</a>).
    </p>

    <p>
      The most quoted interpretations also need testing. Vance attributes the loss mainly to one war, while some Democrats will describe the result as a mandate. Each side may gain politically from its preferred reading, but that does not settle which reading is accurate, and both should be judged against voter-level evidence when it arrives. About 4 in 10 voters in the AP poll said Trump was not a factor in their vote at all (<a href="#ref-ap" class="citation-link">AP Voter Poll 2026</a>), which is a reminder that a referendum reading does not describe every voter.
    </p>

    <p>
      The map also contains exceptions. Byron Donalds won the Florida governor's race (<a href="#ref-elkind" class="citation-link">Elkind 2026</a>). Cuban-majority precincts stayed Republican. These cases do not undo the broader pattern, but they show that the wave was neither uniform nor a collapse of Republican identity. Republicans lost a large number of conditional supporters and kept a core. That distinction is exactly what makes the coming years hard to predict.
    </p>

  </section>

      <h2>What Comes Next</h2>
    </div>

    <div class="primer-card">
      <div class="primer-card-header">
        <span class="primer-card-badge">💡 The Big Picture</span>
      </div>
      <div class="primer-card-title">What can a Democratic Congress actually do with a Republican president?</div>
      <div class="primer-card-body">
        Not as much as you might think. Trump can veto bills, and Democrats don't have enough senators to override him or to get around the filibuster. Their real power is investigating the administration, controlling the budget and approving (or blocking) nominees. Both parties now face internal fights, and the pattern of recent elections suggests that whoever holds power next will face the same angry voters.
        <div class="primer-analogy">
          <strong>Key Concepts:</strong> The <span class="term-chip" data-term="filibuster">filibuster</span> and the veto limit new laws. <span class="term-chip" data-term="oversight">Oversight</span> and the <span class="term-chip" data-term="war_powers">War Powers Resolution</span> are where the fights will happen.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <p>
      Very little Democratic legislation is likely to become law before 2029. Hakeem Jeffries planned to bring bills within 100 hours to restore Affordable Care Act subsidies, reverse Medicaid cuts, end the tariffs and force an end to the Iran war or a vote to authorize it (<a href="#ref-yahoo" class="citation-link">Yahoo News 2026</a>). With 56 senators at best, Democrats cannot break a <span class="term-chip" data-term="filibuster">filibuster</span>, let alone override a veto. These bills will work as messages. The real power of this majority lies in appropriations, subpoenas and Senate confirmations, including any Supreme Court vacancy that opens in the next two years.
    </p>

    <p>
      <span class="term-chip" data-term="oversight">Oversight</span> also has a clearer mandate than impeachment. About 7 in 10 voters said Congress is doing too little on checks and balances (<a href="#ref-ap" class="citation-link">AP Voter Poll 2026</a>). Democrats were already weighing impeachment before the election (<a href="#ref-yahoo" class="citation-link">Yahoo News 2026</a>), and their base will push hard for it. A conviction would still require 67 senators, so a long run of investigations is likely to come first, with impeachment of a cabinet official being a more likely early fight than impeachment of Trump himself.
    </p>

    <p>
      The early positioning points in that direction. Rep. Robert Garcia, set to chair the House Oversight Committee, has signaled investigations into Trump family business dealings, immigration enforcement, corporate misconduct and the Epstein files (<a href="#ref-foxnews" class="citation-link">Fox News 2026</a>). Jeffries said in September, "We haven't ruled anything in, and we haven't ruled anything out" on impeachment, while Scalise predicted an attempt on "day one" (<a href="#ref-newsweek" class="citation-link">Newsweek 2026</a>). Both statements serve a purpose. Jeffries needs to keep his base and his new members from swing districts in the same caucus, and Scalise needs a threat that can reunite a demoralized party. The first impeachment of 2019 offers a useful warning, since it ended in acquittal and changed few minds. Members who just won Trump districts on the cost of living have little reason to spend 2027 on a trial that cannot succeed.
    </p>

    <p>
      The war will be the sharpest test of congressional power. Rep. Ted Lieu has promised a bill to stop it (<a href="#ref-cbs-b" class="citation-link">CBS News 2026b</a>), and with both chambers in Democratic hands a <span class="term-chip" data-term="war_powers">war powers resolution</span> can now reach the president's desk. Trump has already described his plan for such bills: "I guess I'll become Mr. Veto" (<a href="#ref-newsweek" class="citation-link">Newsweek 2026</a>). There is precedent for the standoff, since he vetoed war powers resolutions on Yemen in 2019 and Iran in 2020. The difference this time is the budget. Congress can attach conditions to defense spending, which raises the likelihood of shutdown confrontations in which Democrats must decide how much of the blame for the next disruption they are willing to risk. Voters who punished one party for rising costs will not necessarily reward the other for a shutdown.
    </p>

    <p>
      Republicans now face the harder identity problem. Mike Johnson is leaving the leadership, Steve Scalise says the party must "be honest" with itself, and Trump answered a question about Vance's 2028 chances with "maybe he won't run" (<a href="#ref-cbs-b" class="citation-link">CBS News 2026b</a>). A president who cannot run again and has just lost swing voters holds less over members who must face those voters in 2028. Republicans in competitive seats will likely break with him first on tariffs and war powers, since those are the issues where the exit polls give them cover. Vance's post may preview a central question of the coming primary: was 2026 a referendum on one war or on Trumpism itself? His post favors the first answer. His problem is that the president he serves still controls the war.
    </p>

    <p>
      Democrats have their own fight waiting. Their class of winners includes Roy Cooper and Sherrod Brown, along with progressives like Michigan House winner William Lawrence, and Nick Beauchamp has already framed Michigan as a bellwether for the dispute between the left and the center-left (<a href="#ref-northeastern" class="citation-link">Northeastern Global News 2026</a>; <a href="#ref-cbs-b" class="citation-link">CBS News 2026b</a>). Both wings will claim the win, and neither has much evidence that voters chose its program. Brown also has less time than his colleagues. Ohio's race was a special election for Vance's old seat, so he faces voters again in 2028, potentially with Vance at the top of the Republican ticket. Ohio may be the first test of whether the loan was renewed.
    </p>

    <p>
      Democrats are likely to enter 2028 as favorites for the White House, for a slightly uncomfortable reason. Divided government lets them criticize while Trump still owns prices and the war. Christopher Wlezien's (<a href="#ref-wlezien" class="citation-link">1995</a>) <span class="term-chip" data-term="thermostatic_model">thermostatic model</span> predicts that public opinion moves against whoever holds power, and recent history fits: the White House changed parties in 2016, 2020 and 2024, and the House has now flipped against the sitting president in 2018, 2022 and 2026. The closest precedent is 2006, when an unpopular war in a president's second term gave Democrats Congress and preceded Barack Obama's win. Democrats then lost 63 House seats in 2010. If they win in 2028, the same voters who moved this year will be holding them responsible for the cost of living by 2030, and senators from Texas, Kansas and South Carolina will be seeking reelection in 2032 with no Trump to run against.
    </p>

    <div class="case-card" style="margin-top: 2rem; border-left: 4px solid var(--accent-color); background: var(--bg-card);">
      <div class="case-card-header">
        <span class="case-card-title">Conclusion</span>
        <span class="case-card-tag">The Road to 2028</span>
      </div>
      <p>
        The 2026 midterms suggest that a meaningful share of the voters Trump had counted as his own turned against his party. Prices, a war felt at the gas pump, and a sense that the administration served itself appear to have pushed voters across party lines in numbers large enough to overwhelm the maps drawn to contain them, though turnout, candidate quality and local conditions also shaped the result. Those voters gave Democrats real power without giving them much trust, and that gap is likely to be the most important fact of the next two years. Trump was not formally on the ballot, but his leadership and agenda were part of the electoral choice. The results suggest that they were a liability in important contests, even if they were not the only cause of Republican losses. In 2028 he cannot be on the ballot at all, for the first time in a presidential race since 2012. Both parties will then have to find out what they are without him.
      </p>
